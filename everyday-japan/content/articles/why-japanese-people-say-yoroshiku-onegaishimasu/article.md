# Why Japanese People Say Yoroshiku Onegaishimasu

You introduce yourself at a small office. Names, a slight bow, a business card held with both hands. Then, almost as punctuation, someone says **yoroshiku onegaishimasu (よろしくお願いします)**.

You hear it again at the end of an email, after a request to a hotel, when a new teammate joins a group chat. It is not “hello.” It is not exactly “please.” It does not map cleanly onto one English verb.

Why do Japanese people say yoroshiku onegaishimasu so often?

The useful answer is about a social job, not a mystic untranslatable. The phrase asks for decent future treatment and offers it back. It closes a first contact the way a handshake might close one elsewhere—except the body language beside it is often a [bow rather than a handshake](/why-japanese-people-bow-instead-of-shaking-hands). You can live in Japan without saying it perfectly. You will hear it anyway.

---

## The Visitor Expectation Versus the Closer

English speakers look for a translation they can reuse. “Nice to meet you” covers the first minute. “Please take care of this” covers a task. “I look forward to working with you” covers a job. Yoroshiku onegaishimasu can sit in all of those chairs, and in none of them exactly.

That overlap tempts a tourist-book claim: the word cannot be translated. That claim is too strong. Plenty of languages have formulas that bundle goodwill, request, and future relation. English uses several sentences where Japanese often uses one set phrase. The difficulty is matching, not magic.

Visitors also confuse it with neighboring formulas. **Sumimasen** apologizes or gets attention. **Daijoubu** manages whether something is all right. **Onegaishimasu** alone can mean “please (do this)” to a clerk. Yoroshiku onegaishimasu is the relational cousin: please treat what comes next—and me, in it—favorably. It is closer to “I’m counting on a decent relationship” than to “pass the salt.”

You will hear shorter forms: **yoroshiku**, **yoroshiku ne**, **yoroshiku onegai shimasu**, the more formal **yoroshiku onegai itashimasu**. Register changes with age, rank, and email. The job stays similar.

This is not uniquely Japanese in the sense that other cultures lack closing formulas. It is Japanese in its frequency at small thresholds: the start of a group project, the end of a polite message, the moment after names. Missing it in a formal first meeting can sound unfinished, the way walking away without “nice to meet you” can sound unfinished in English. Missing it at a convenience-store counter is fine. The clerk’s onegaishimasu is a different, shorter tool.

## How Japanese People Use It Without Thinking About Translation

Growing up in Japan, many children hear yoroshiku when they join a sports club, a classroom group, or a neighbor’s introduction. The adult version at work is stiffer, but the shape is familiar: we will share time, so we name that fact.

In person, it often rides with a bow and a name. In a chat, it may be a sticker or a single line after “I’ll be joining from April.” In email, it is a standard closer, sometimes almost empty, like “best regards”—except it still carries a faint request: please handle this thread kindly.

Locals do not pause to feel the poetry. They notice when it is missing in a context that expects it, or when it is piled on so thick it sounds oily. Overuse in a hard sales pitch can feel like pressure. Underuse in a first team meeting can feel blunt. The phrase is ordinary until the register is wrong.

It also appears when you ask a favor. You explain the request, then yoroshiku onegaishimasu. Here it leans toward “please look after this.” That is why learners glue it to “please” and then overuse it at restaurants. A waiter needs a clear order. A new colleague needs a relational closer. Same family of words, different jobs.

People who [avoid a flat no](/why-japanese-people-avoid-saying-no-directly) sometimes wrap a difficult ask in extra yoroshiku. That can be considerate or evasive, depending on whether the actual content is clear. The phrase does not replace information. It cushions the start of a relationship around the information.

Compared with [sumimasen](/why-japanese-people-say-sumimasen-so-often), yoroshiku is forward-looking. Sumimasen often marks a bump in the present: excuse me, sorry, thank you for the trouble. Yoroshiku marks a stretch of future: we will be in contact; please let that go well. You can hear both in one introduction—sorry for interrupting, then yoroshiku.

Gift-giving and greetings sometimes travel together. A small souvenir on a first visit is not required, but when it happens, the words around it may include yoroshiku: please treat our connection as something to be handled with care. That is adjacent to [why gifts appear so often](/why-japanese-people-give-gifts-so-often), not a translation of the box.

## Why a Relational Closer Became Standard

**First meetings as unfinished business.** Japanese workplaces, schools, and neighborhood associations often assume ongoing contact, not a one-scene encounter. A phrase that points at the future fits that assumption. **Fact:** the formula is taught and expected in many introductions. **Interpretation:** this is not proof that Japanese people are uniquely harmonious. It is a linguistic habit for naming that you are not done with each other.

**Request culture in polite Japanese.** Onegai shimasu is a grammatical way to ask. Adding yoroshiku (“well / favorably / as appropriate”) turns the ask toward the quality of treatment, not only the action. Email inherited this from letters. The closer survived because it is safe: it does not commit you to a specific promise, and it does not refuse.

**Hierarchy and groups.** When a junior joins, yoroshiku can mean “please be patient with me.” When a senior says it, it can mean “I expect we will work smoothly,” which is warmer or heavier depending on tone. The same words, different weight. Visitors who learn one movie-subtitle meaning will mis-hear rank.

**Not a substitute for clarity.** Modern companies still need deadlines and yes/no. The phrase can paper over a vague request. Japanese speakers complain about this too. A good email is specific, then yoroshiku. A poor email is only yoroshiku.

**Limits of the “untranslatable” story.** Translation studies and learners’ books love the gap. The gap is real as a one-to-one dictionary problem. It is overstated as a cultural wall. You can explain the job in English in a sentence or two. You cannot always replace it with one word at the end of a message and keep the same flavor. That is a normal fact about formulas, including English ones that puzzle Japanese learners (“cheers,” “love,” “regards”).

**Where it is weak or absent.** Close friends may drop it or joke with it. Some young chat styles shorten everything. Customer-service scripts use other set lines. Rural, urban, corporate, and freelance scenes do not intone it identically. Night-life introductions may skip the whole package. The phrase is common, not compulsory oxygen.

**Packaging of the self.** A first meeting is a small presentation. Names, bows, cards, sometimes a wrapped gift. Yoroshiku is the verbal wrap: please receive this person neatly. [Careful packaging](/why-japanese-packaging-is-so-careful) is a different topic, but the instinct to finish an offering so it is easy to receive is not a wild comparison—as long as it stays a comparison, not a theory of the nation.

## Email, Shops, and the Wrong Please

Visitors paste yoroshiku onegaishimasu under every request because a textbook put it next to “please.”

At a ticket window, “onegaishimasu” after the destination is enough. At a first meeting, the longer phrase earns its keep. In a complaint email, leading with yoroshiku and never stating the problem is backwards. In a thank-you note after someone already helped you, a simple thanks may be cleaner than another yoroshiku, which can sound like you are asking again.

Machine translation often spits “Nice to meet you” or “Thank you in advance.” Both are sometimes right and often slightly wrong. “Thank you in advance” can sound presumptuous in English; yoroshiku is more like placing the relationship on the table. If you need an English line in a bilingual office, “I look forward to working with you” or “I’d appreciate your help with this” is usually closer than a word-for-word.

You do not need to bow while typing. You do need to match formality: itashimasu in a careful message to a client; a shorter yoroshiku among equals who already said it in person.

## Real-Life Scenarios Visitors Notice

**A classroom or club.** New members face the group, names, then a chorus of yoroshiku.

**A job’s first day.** The phrase appears in the self-introduction, then again in the follow-up email.

**A group chat.** “I’ll handle Friday. Yoroshiku.” Here it means please count on it / please go along with this plan.

**A hotel request.** “Early check-in, yoroshiku onegaishimasu.” Leans toward please treat this ask well.

**A neighbor introduction.** Moving in, a brief visit, maybe sweets, yoroshiku. Future noise and trash days are the real content.

**A shop.** Staff say onegaishimasu; you may not need the full yoroshiku unless you are starting a longer custom order.

**A refusal wrapped in softness.** Lots of yoroshiku, little yes. Listen to the middle of the message, not only the ribbon.

## Visitor Tips for Using the Phrase

**Use it at first meetings and at the end of polite requests.** That covers most of what you will need.

**Do not bolt it onto every “please.”** Order food plainly. Save the longer formula for people you will see again or for written asks.

**Match the formality you hear.** Copy the workplace’s email closer rather than the textbook’s longest version.

**If you speak English in a Japanese office, a clear English closer is better than a broken yoroshiku.** Effort is appreciated; muddle is not required.

**Do not treat it as a spell that makes a vague email polite.** Dates, names, and what you want still matter.

**Hear it as forward-looking.** If you need to apologize for a bump right now, sumimasen is the closer tool.

**A small bow in person is enough.** You do not need a speech about Japanese culture.

**If you forget it, a sincere “nice to meet you” plus a name is not a crisis.** The next email can include the formula if the relationship continues.

## Yoroshiku Onegaishimasu FAQ

### What does it literally mean?

Roughly “I request that things go favorably” or “please treat this/me well.” Literal word-by-word glosses sound stiff because the phrase is a formula.

### Is it untranslatable?

It has no single perfect English twin. It is translatable as a job: a relational closer and a soft request about future treatment. Avoid the romance of “cannot be said in English.”

### Is it the same as please?

Sometimes it overlaps with a request. It is not the everyday please for ordering coffee.

### Is it the same as sumimasen?

No. Sumimasen marks trouble, thanks, or attention in the present. Yoroshiku points at what comes next.

### Do I have to say it?

In many first-meeting and email contexts it is expected. In casual shopping, no. Listen to the room.

### Can it sound pushy?

Yes, if it assumes a yes you have not received, or if it floods a sales pitch. Tone and content still count.

### Should I write it in every email?

Many work emails use it. If you already said it, and the thread is purely logistical, a short thanks can be enough. Follow the local pattern.

## A Closer, Not a Mystery

Japanese people say yoroshiku onegaishimasu often because a lot of daily contact is the start of ongoing contact, and the language keeps a standard way to ask that it go decently. That is a formula with a job, not a national soul.

When you hear it after a name and a bow, you are being invited into a stretch of time, not into a riddle. Answer with your name, a matching level of care, and a clear next sentence about what you actually need. The phrase will do the rest of its small work without requiring you to worship it.

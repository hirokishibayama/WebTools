# Why Japanese People Say Itadakimasu

The tray is set. Rice, soup, a side dish. For a second nobody moves. Someone says **itadakimasu (いただきます)**, a few people murmur it back, and then chopsticks lift.

To a visitor, the pause can look like grace before a meal—heads slightly down, hands together. To many Japanese families it is closer to “let’s eat,” with a layer of thanks folded in. It is ordinary. It is not a test of your soul.

Why do Japanese people say *itadakimasu*?

The short answer is manners plus gratitude language, taught hard in childhood—especially at **kyūshoku (給食)**, school lunch—and then kept, loosely, in adult life. It is not exactly a religious prayer for most speakers, even if the word’s history brushes against Buddhist vocabulary. After the meal, **gochisōsama (ごちそうさま)** does the closing job. Visitors are not required to perform it. Locals often appreciate the attempt and rarely police a skip.

---

## What Visitors Are Actually Seeing

*Itadakimasu* is the humble form of “to receive.” In a meal context it means something like “I receive this food.” The object of the thanks is blurry on purpose: the cook, the ingredients, the person who paid, the fact that there is a meal at all. That blur is why travel writing can inflate it into a grand speech about every fish and farmer. Everyday speech is smaller. Kids say it because the table does not start until someone does. Office workers say it over a [convenience-store](/why-japanese-convenience-stores-feel-so-different) bento at their desk, sometimes under their breath, sometimes not at all.

Hands may meet in a light **gasshō (合掌)** — palms together — especially for children. Adults often skip the hands and keep the word. Television characters say it as a sitcom beat. None of this is a liturgy with a fixed creed.

**Fact vs interpretation:** You can describe a historical path from Buddhist receiving-language and from older table manners. You cannot honestly claim that the average commuter is praying. For many people it is a politeness chime, like saying “thanks for cooking” without naming anyone.

It is also easy to overstate uniformity. Some households almost never say it. Some say it only when guests are present. Some say it for home cooking and skip it for a snack. Solo eaters may feel silly announcing it to an empty room and still mouth it out of habit. Solo eaters may also just eat. Both are Japanese life.

## How Children Learn the Start Button

School lunch is the loudest classroom. In many elementary schools, the class waits, a student or teacher cues the phrase, and the room says *itadakimasu* together before anyone starts. Staff often frame it as starting together, not wasting food, and noticing that someone prepared the meal. In some classrooms that sits inside **shokuiku (食育)** — food education — and a few teachers use stronger language about lives that became the meal. That is school talk about waste and thanks, not a required doctrine at the family table. Serving roles already make lunch a group task; the phrase is the audible start line.

At home, parents prompt toddlers the way they prompt *please*. The child who reaches for a strawberry first gets a reminder. The habit sticks unevenly after high school. University students sharing a cheap meal may still chorus it. A tired adult over a midnight bowl of noodles may not.

Japanese people often talk about **arigatai (ありがたい)** — a feeling that something is a gift, not an entitlement — without turning dinner into a sermon. *Itadakimasu* is one of the small places that feeling is allowed to be brief. Then you eat, because the soup gets cold.

The phrase also manages social start-times. In a group, eating before the senior person or before the shared “go” can look greedy. *Itadakimasu* is a coordinated green light. That function is easy to miss if you only hear the translation “I humbly receive.”

When someone else cooked, the thanks has a clearer target. When you cooked for yourself, the word can feel slightly theatrical. People still say it. Habit does not always need a perfect audience.

## Not a Required Religious Prayer, Not Meaningless Either

Several threads sit underneath the phrase without turning it into a temple ritual.

**Humble receiving.** Japanese has a whole toolkit of humble and honorific verbs. *Itadaku* belongs to that toolkit. Using it at the table marks the food as something received, not seized. That is linguistic manners. It is related, loosely, to other everyday softeners—the same world in which people [say *sumimasen*](/why-japanese-people-say-sumimasen-so-often) for small intrusions—but it is not the same word and not the same job.

**Religious vocabulary without required belief.** Buddhist and other religious settings use receiving-language too. Some families put hands together in a way that looks like prayer. Many speakers are not performing a doctrine. Treating every *itadakimasu* as Buddhist practice is like treating “bless you” after a sneeze as a full theology exam. Some people mean more. Many mean less. Ask, if it matters; do not assume.

**School as a manners factory.** The humble verb and the table phrase are older than anyone now eating kyūshoku. What school lunch did, in many districts, was make a group recitation loud and daily. That habit explains the volume of the adult phrase better than an unbroken ancient rule. Exact curriculum language varies by school and era; lots of adults learned the chorus in a cafeteria, not at a shrine.

**Gochisōsama as the pair.** After eating, **gochisōsama deshita** thanks the meal and, by extension, the host or cook. Leaving without it after a hosted dinner is ruder than skipping *itadakimasu* as a foreign guest. In a restaurant, people may say it toward staff as they leave, or only toward the table. [Tipping is still uncommon](/why-japanese-people-rarely-tip-at-restaurants); the verbal close is not a substitute cash tip. It is a different channel.

**Hosted meals sit nearby.** A visitor who has already noticed [gift-giving](/why-japanese-people-give-gifts-so-often) sometimes folds *itadakimasu* into a grand theory of Japanese gratitude. The safer account is narrower: meals have a start-phrase and an end-phrase, taught early, used unevenly, appreciated when guests try. Being fed in someone’s home is closer to receiving a gift than to a restaurant transaction, which is why the phrases show up more clearly there.

**Interpretation:** *Itadakimasu* is a conventional pre-meal utterance that can carry thanks, coordination, and childhood memory. It is table manners with optional feeling—not a stand-in for a religious rite, and not an empty noise.

## When People Skip It, Mumble It, or Only Do the Hands

Honesty requires the messy usage.

**Snacks, ice cream, convenience-store bites.** Many people do not announce a full *itadakimasu* for a single rice ball on a bench. Some still do. There is no exam.

**Drinking parties.** The group may toast with **kanpai (乾杯)** instead. Food arrives in waves. The textbook pause dissolves into “go ahead.”

**Foreign restaurants in Japan.** Some diners keep the phrase. Some switch to the other culture’s habit. Neither is a betrayal.

**Hospital trays and airplane meals.** Some people still whisper it. Some feel the audience is a plastic lid and skip it. Habit is not a courtroom.

**People who dislike formality.** They eat. Relatives may tease them. Nobody is grading it.

**Filmed “authentic experiences.”** Tourist workshops can over-coach a slow, solemn *itadakimasu* that looks unlike a weekday kitchen. Match the room you are actually in.

**Religious households.** A Buddhist home may add more. A Christian family in Japan may say a different grace, or both, or neither. Japan is not a single altar.

If you only watch a TV family drama, everyone speaks in unison with shining eyes. If you only watch a silent solo diner at 10 p.m., you will think the custom is dead. Weekday life sits in the middle: a short word, then food.

## Real-Life Scenarios Visitors Notice

**Elementary school, 12:20 p.m.** Trays down. A cue. A roomful of *itadakimasu*. Then noise.

**A home dinner with guests.** Hosts may wait for you. Saying it once, clearly, is enough. A three-minute speech is too much.

**An izakaya.** Dishes land whenever they are ready. People start after a short “go ahead.” The textbook pause is gone.

**A packed lunch on a park bench.** A mumbled word, or none. Pigeons do not care.

**Leaving a family restaurant.** *Gochisōsama* toward the staff at the register. No extra cash on the tray.

**A cooking class for visitors.** The instructor asks everyone to say it. This is pedagogy, not a measure of your respect for Japan.

## Visitor Tips Without Over-Mystifying Yourself

**You are not required to say it.** Especially as a guest who was not taught the rhythm. A smile and “thank you” in English or Japanese is understood.

**If you want to join, keep it short.** One *itadakimasu* at the start. One *gochisōsama deshita* when finished or when leaving a hosted table.

**Do not perform a temple ritual at a conveyor-belt sushi counter** unless the people you are with are doing something similar. Match volume and length to the table.

**Do not correct Japanese people who skip it.** Their table, their register.

**Hands together are optional.** Copy the host. Do not invent extra bows.

**If you cooked, you can still say it.** Some people feel odd thanking themselves; others keep the habit as a start chime. Either is fine.

**After a hosted meal, the closing phrase matters more than perfect pronunciation of the opening one.** Thank the cook.

**Do not turn leftovers lectures into your personality.** Reducing waste is a real value for many households. Preaching it as the secret meaning of *itadakimasu* is still preaching.

## Itadakimasu in Japan FAQ

### Is *itadakimasu* a prayer?

For most people in daily life, no—not in the sense of addressing a deity with a set belief. It can look prayer-like. Meaning ranges from habit to sincere thanks.

### Do I have to say it in restaurants?

No. Staff will not usually wait for your recitation. Some diners still say it quietly.

### What should I say after eating?

**Gochisōsama deshita** is the common closer, especially toward a host or as you leave.

### Why do schoolchildren say it together?

Coordination, manners education, and noticing the meal as a shared event—not a national religion class in every cafeteria.

### Is it rude if a visitor does not say it?

Usually not, if you are otherwise polite. At a homestay, following the family once is a kind gesture.

### Can I say it if I am not Japanese?

Yes. It is a Japanese sentence, not a restricted rite. Keep it ordinary. Avoid theatrical slow motion.

### Does everyone in Japan say it every time?

No. Age, setting, personality, and whether anyone cooked for you all change the odds.

## A Start Word, Then the Soup Gets Cold

Japanese people often say *itadakimasu* because childhood tables and school lunch taught a shared start, and because the language already has a humble verb for receiving. It can hold thanks for the cook and the ingredients. For most speakers it is not a religious service, and it is not a required line that makes the meal authentic.

Say it if the table is saying it. Skip it if you would only be performing for a camera in your head. Eat while the food is still food. The phrase was always in service of that, not the other way around.

If you remember only one distinction, remember this: *itadakimasu* starts the meal; *gochisōsama* thanks it. Neither one is a test that proves you understood Japan. They are table words. Use them as table words.
